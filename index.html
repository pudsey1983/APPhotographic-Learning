How are these results calculated?

Standard thin-lens depth-of-field equations are used. Hyperfocal distance: H = f² / (N × c) + f, where f is focal length, N is f-number and c is the sensor-format circle of confusion. Near and far limits are calculated from H and the subject distance. Results are estimates; acceptable sharpness depends on print size, viewing distance and personal judgement. The photographic preview and zone diagram are conceptual rather than physical-scale or calibrated lens simulations.

How does the flash calculator work?

At full power and ISO 100, guide number (metres) ≈ aperture × direct flash distance. Effective guide number scales with the square root of relative flash power and ISO. A conceptual two-stop loss is applied for bounce. Flash strength is estimated in exposure stops; ambient exposure uses scene EV, aperture, shutter and ISO. These are teaching approximations, not calibrated light-meter readings.

Lesson 01

Control the subject with flash

At a constant distance, increasing manual flash power brightens the flash-lit subject. Halving the power reduces flash exposure by one stop.

Lesson 02

Control the background with shutter speed

Within normal flash sync limits, a slower shutter brightens ambient light without substantially increasing the brief flash burst. The subject still receives ambient light too.

Lesson 03

Move the light, change the look

Doubling direct flash distance gives roughly a quarter of the illumination (two stops less). Bounce light can soften shadows but loses power.

Important: Real exposure depends on the speedlight's zoom setting, modifier, room reflectivity, TTL behaviour and camera sync speed. Guide numbers are manufacturer-specific and typically assume direct flash. This tool models ordinary non-HSS manual flash. Check your camera's flash sync limit before using 1/200 or 1/250, and use a test shot or flash meter to confirm exposure.
